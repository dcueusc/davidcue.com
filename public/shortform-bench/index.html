ShortForm-Bench

By David Cue

Short-form video scripts for working creators

Can AI models write short-form video scripts that creators would actually film?

I worked with 25 Instagram and TikTok creators across five niches to test how well frontier AI models can help with their day-to-day content writing. Each model was given a creator's channel, ten of their past scripts and a one-line video idea, then had to research the topic and write the script. The creators read the results blind and decided which ones they would actually film.

Key takeaways

  • Gemini 3.8 Flash leads at 60%. Compared on the same creators and pitches, it is clearly ahead of Claude Haiku 5.5, GPT-6.2 Astra, Gemini 4 Neon and GPT-6 Luna. Its lead over Claude Fable 5.2 (6 points) and Kimi K3.1 Pro (10 points) is within the margin of error, so those models are not separated from it.
  • Small models match flagships on average, but not in every lab. Flagships average 44% usable and small models 47%. A flagship script costs $0.46 on average against $0.06 for a small model, about 7 times as much. The cost figures are measured.
  • Kimi K3.1 Pro is the cheapest model not separated from the leader. It costs $0.08 a script against $0.17 for Gemini 3.8 Flash, or $20 against $41 for all 250 tasks.
  • Accuracy runs from 86% to 92%. Claude Fable 5.2 is the most accurate and GPT-6 Luna the least. The most usable model, Gemini 3.8 Flash, is not the most accurate at 87%: creators approve scripts with errors they don't notice.
  • Models score highest on pacing and lowest on originality. Averaged across models, pacing scores 2.77 out of 4 and originality 2.25.
  • Creators chose a script to film for 222 of 250 pitches. Gemini 3.8 Flash was picked most often, for 65 pitches. For 28 pitches, no model produced a script the creator would film.

About the benchmark

Short-form scripts are hard for AI to write well. A creator wants to give a one-line idea and get back a script that is researched, correct, and sounds like them. Models tend towards an average voice, and an average script is one a creator rewrites or throws away.

A script is only good if the creator would use it, and no automated judge can decide that for them. So the judge here is the creator. Each model writes scripts for real creators' channels, and each creator decides whether they would film what they were given.

The benchmark covers educational content: explainers, lists and advice. These types of videos are structured and rely on factual evidence, making them ideal for evaluation. 25 creators take part, five in each of five niches: personal finance and business, technology, fitness, science, and history and culture. Each has ten pitches, for 250 tasks.

Methodology

Creators and tasks

Creators were picked from TikTok and Instagram through a combination of engagement metrics and content analysis. The resulting lists were then manually verified to meet the requirements of the study. A creator qualifies if they are an individual or small team making their own scripted, spoken, educational videos in English, 20 to 180 seconds long, with at least ten structured explainers, lists or advice videos among their recent posts. Companies, clip aggregators, reaction channels and mass-produced AI content are excluded.

A task is one creator plus one pitch. Each pitch is a video idea the creator approved for their own channel and agreed to film. The channel descriptions and pitches were first drafted from each creator's past videos, then checked and approved by the creator. Creators agreed in writing to the use of their transcripts, are paid, and are not named.

Part of the taskWhat it is
Channel descriptionUp to 150 words on who watches, what the channel covers and how the creator sounds
Past scriptsTen of the creator's published videos, as transcripts, each with its length in seconds
PitchA title and one or two sentences on a video the creator hasn't made, with a target length in seconds
ToolsWeb search and page fetch, the same for every model, up to 20 calls
OutputA script of spoken lines only, written line by line, plus the pages it relied on

How scripts are generated

Every model gets the same plain prompt: who the script is for, what it is given, the rules for the script, and the reply format. The prompt carries no writing advice and is not tuned for any model. The model never sees the creator's name, links, captions or view counts, so it can't look them up.

Research tools run on our side, not the model provider's. Each model's built-in web search stays off, so every model searches the same index and reads pages through the same reader; otherwise the benchmark would partly rank search products. Within a round, the first result for a search or page is saved and reused, so two models that ask the same thing get the same answer. Search uses Google results through Serper, called via the Composio command-line tool. Pages are read by our own reader; when a site refuses it, Serper's page reader is used instead.

Each model writes one script per task. There are no second attempts and no picking the best of several. A request is retried only when the service fails, never because of what a model wrote. Every model is called through one API with the same settings, and the script is never edited before the creator sees it.

Scripts are written line by line

Short-form creators write and read scripts one line at a time. Models are asked for one spoken line on each line of text, each a single beat said in one breath, with no paragraphs, headings, stage directions or on-screen text. The examples above show what comes back. Length is given the way creators think about it, as a target in seconds, not a word count.

How creators rate

Each creator reads every script written for their own pitches. Scripts are unlabelled and in shuffled order, so the creator doesn't know which model wrote which. They give each script an overall rating and five sub-scores.

Overall: would I film this?

1234

4 Film as is. 3 Small edits, faster than writing it myself. 2 Heavy rewrite. 1 Reject.

A script rated 3 or 4 is usable.

Five sub-scores, each 1 to 4

Voice. Does it sound like me?
Hook. Would the first lines stop someone scrolling?
Substance. Is it specific, interesting and correct as far as I can tell?
Pacing. Read line by line, does it move well?
Originality. Is there an angle or line I wouldn't have thought of, or does it read as generic?

For each pitch, the creator then films and posts one of the scripts they rated usable. Filming is the check on the rating: a 3 or 4 is a commitment to shoot.

Accuracy

A creator can approve a script with an error they didn't notice, so accuracy is measured separately. Each model lists the pages its script relies on. An automated checker pulls the factual claims out of the script and compares each with the saved copy of the page the model cited. Accuracy is the share of claims the cited page supports. A claim with no source counts as unsupported. Accuracy was checked using Claude Opus 5.5, which looked at the cited sources and confirmed whether they matched the script's claims.

Scoring and uncertainty

Usable rate is the share of a model's scripts rated 3 or 4. It is worked out for each creator and then averaged, so every creator counts equally. Sub-scores are averaged the same way and are not combined into one number.

Every score is shown with its standard error: how far it would typically move if the benchmark were rerun with a different set of creators. It is calculated across creators, not across single ratings, because ten scripts judged by the same person are not independent.

To decide whether one model is really ahead of another, the two are compared on the same creators and pitches. A tough rater or a hard pitch affects both models equally, so it cancels out, and the gap between two models is known more precisely than either model's own score. Each gap is reported with a 95% confidence interval, found by resampling whole creators. A model counts as behind the leader only when that interval lies entirely above zero.

Models and settings

Limitations

  • Small panel. 25 creators can separate models that differ by about 10 points, not by 2 or 3.
  • Words only. Delivery, visuals and editing are not tested, and some creators teach partly through what is on screen.
  • Willing creators. People who agree to take part may be more open to AI scripts than creators in general.
  • One person's view. Each script is rated by one creator, and the sub-scores come from that same person.
  • Reading several takes on one pitch. Models often land on the same sources and a similar script, so later versions can feel repetitive. Order is shuffled so this falls evenly across models.
  • Automated accuracy. The checker can miss an unsupported claim or wrongly flag a supported one.
  • The web changes. Models research live, so a rerun months later will not find the same pages.

Data and code

The tasks, scripts and creator ratings are kept private so models can't be trained on them, apart from the three example pitches shown above. The generation harness, the exact prompt and the tools are public at github.com/dcue3/shortform-bench, along with the three example tasks and every model's script for them. The harness is plain Python with no dependencies, and records the full conversation, every tool call, token counts, cost and timings for each script.
